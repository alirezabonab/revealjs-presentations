E2E Group · Platform Proposal 2026-08-21 Draft for discussion Internal Author: Alireza Bonab

The Missing Link

Connecting AI harnesses to our data and our actions — a governed MCP platform for an AI-native E2E.

The proposal in one sentence: every team that owns data, a service, or a system makes it agent-accessible through an MCP it owns; a shared identity layer and a thin gateway make that access governed, scoped, and audited; and on top of it, anyone in the organisation can get dashboards and durable automations by describing what they need.

01 · Why now

Built for two, asked by everyone

Our core services were built around two audiences: the customer platform and the sales team. Those two have integrated services and own their domain APIs. Finance, Compliance, Marketing, Customer Support and the banking back office have no integrated services at all. They run their day-to-day work in third-party SaaS tools, and there is no path from those tools into our core platform.

So every time they need something from it, they come to product and dev: give me access to this data, build me this report, automate this job, reconcile these two systems, send this notification. Each request becomes a ticket, a context switch for an engineer, and a two-week wait for a ten-minute job. Today the dev backlog is the only door into the platform — and that is the bottleneck this proposal removes.

At the same time, the tools that could serve that demand have quietly become capable. AI harnesses — Claude Code, Codex, and whatever comes next — are no longer novelty chatbots but orchestrators: they understand intent, discover tools at runtime, plan and compose calls across systems, recover from transient failures, and stop to ask before doing anything consequential. That last property is what makes the rest of this proposal safe. The reasoning half of the problem is already solved for us by vendors; we do not have to build it.

The missing piece is not intelligence. It is access. There is no governed path between the harnesses and the things they would read or act on. So today, value leaks through the worst possible channel: copy-paste into chat windows, ad-hoc credentials, one-off scripts on laptops — exactly the pattern our AI policy exists to prevent, happening informally because no sanctioned path exists.

The organisations that win the next five years are the ones where an employee's request reaches what it needs with the least friction — without giving up control. The range matters more than the phrasing: a question, a report, an action inside a system, a job that keeps running every week. Not just asking things — doing them too. That is what "AI-native" means in practice: not more chatbots, but every asset we own reachable through a governed interface, with identity — not habit — deciding who can do what.

02 · What we already have

Five building blocks, one identity — and no shared authorization

We are not starting from zero. The raw material for an AI-native organisation already exists. Four estates hold the data, the endpoints, the tools and the runs — and a fifth building block holds the thing none of them contain, which is what any of it means in business terms.

Data

Data Platform (GCP / BigQuery)

Collects transactional log data from services and builds versioned records of every row — we can replay how any record changed over time.

Agent value: the perfect substrate for questions and dashboards. "How did this change over time?" is a first-class query, already built.

Services

~80 event-driven services

Event-driven messaging architecture with eventual consistency. Each service owns its domain, its data, and in some cases its own local roles.

Agent value: domain lookups and guarded actions. Caveat: reads may trail writes — tool descriptions must say so, and MCPs must expose it honestly.

Systems

SaaS systems & tooling

Marketing platforms, SMS & email, monitoring and log gathering, debugging tools — the operational glue, mostly behind vendor APIs.

Agent value: observability answers ("why is this failing?") and communication actions ("notify these customers") — the highest-frequency requests we field.

Execution

Windmill

Our workflow orchestration platform: scripts, flows, schedules, retries, approval steps — durable execution we already trust.

Agent value: the execution plane the harness lacks. The harness plans; Windmill runs things durably and deterministically.

Knowledge

The knowledge graph (agent-skills)

A markdown context library, versioned in git: business capabilities, products, journeys, systems, governance and ways of working — scoped Shared / Enklare / Entra, every file with a named owner and a review date, and an access matrix defining which use case may read which file.

Agent value: this is what an MCP reads to turn a business request into the right calls. It also carries the separation rule — Enklare context must not be used to infer Entra lending, credit or funding rules — so entity separation is enforced by the model, not left to a model's judgement.

The distinction is worth being precise about, because it is the one that makes the other four usable. The four estates answer "here are the rows" or "here is the endpoint". Nothing in them can answer "what does this company mean by lead quality, where does it live, and whose rules apply to it?" — and without that, an agent guesses at our vocabulary and returns something plausible and wrong. The knowledge graph is the cheapest block on this list, and it already has its own workstream and its own governance.

The identity reality

Everyone signs in through Google Workspace — so we have a single, strong identity for every person. But we have no organisation-wide role and access model. Some services carry domain-local roles; they don't compose, and nothing outside those services can reason about them.

The honest blocker

The missing piece is not AI tooling — it is authorization. Any serious agent platform forces us to answer "who may see what, who may do what" in a machine-readable way. That investment is overdue regardless of AI: it pays off for every integration, every audit, and every DORA conversation we will ever have. This proposal treats it as the foundation, not a footnote.

03 · The proposal

Three pillars, one operating principle

The proposal is deliberately boring at its core: standard protocols, thin infrastructure, and ownership pushed to the teams that already own the assets.

Pillar 1

Domain MCPs, owned by owning teams

Each team that owns data, a service domain, or a system builds and maintains an MCP server for it — and carries it on their roadmap like any other product surface.

Per domain, not per service: ~80 services does not mean 80 MCPs — realistically 8–12 cover the estate that matters, plus one over the knowledge graph. The MCP owns model-friendly tools and schemas, domain rules, tool-level authorization, ownership checks, PII redaction, and side-effect classification. "Done" starts to include "agent-accessible".

Pillar 2

Identity & authorization foundation

Google Workspace stays the IdP. We add an OAuth authorization server in front of it: token issuance, refresh, revocation, and — critically — token exchange into short-lived, audience-bound tokens per domain.

Roles v1 is pragmatic: Google Workspace groups mapped to scopes. A user's token can only ever be exchanged for the domains their groups grant, and the token the harness holds is itself bound to the gateway — it never reaches a domain MCP. No standing credentials anywhere in the chain.

Pillar 3

A thin MCP gateway

One endpoint that harnesses connect to. It aggregates and routes tool calls, applies platform policy and rate limits, handles name collisions, and writes the central audit and trace log.

Thin is a feature: no workflow planning, no business logic, no domain knowledge. Everything smart lives in the harness (orchestration) or the domain MCP (rules).

Operating principle

The harness orchestrates. The gateway routes. The domain MCP guards. Windmill executes durably. Every layer does one job, which is what keeps the system auditable, testable, and replaceable — including replacing the harness vendor, because MCP is an open standard supported across the ecosystem.

04 · Target architecture

One picture: identity in, audited action out

One request, end to end

The trust chain matters more than the boxes, and the important detail is that the gateway swaps tokens rather than forwarding them. You connect the gateway in the harness once and sign in with Workspace. The harness then holds one token whose audience is the gateway and nothing else. On every call after that, the harness sends that token to the gateway, the gateway checks it, and Auth mints a new short-lived token whose audience is exactly one domain MCP. The gateway sends the new token onward.

The user token never leaves the gateway. Each domain MCP only ever sees a token minted for it, and checks that too — signature, expiry, scopes, and "was this minted for me?". A token minted for the Data Platform is useless against Windmill or the comms system. That is token exchange, not forwarding. Nothing in the chain holds standing credentials on behalf of "the AI", the user never logs in again, domain MCPs never show a login screen, and revoking the person in Workspace kills every token in the chain.

05 · What we get

Where the value lands

The pain is not in dispute, so this section is about what the platform is worth rather than what it fixes. It is worth different things to the two halves of the organisation, and both need to hold for this to be a platform investment rather than a productivity experiment.

For the people

The work stops queueing

Answers in minutes, in their own words, without opening a ticket. They build the view they need and change it just by asking, instead of specifying it to someone else and waiting. The repeating work runs itself and keeps running.

And the access model is a benefit here, not a restriction: because scopes follow their role, people can use this confidently without wondering whether they are allowed to be looking at something. That is what makes adoption stick rather than quietly reverting to asking a colleague.

For the organisation

Capacity back, and a foundation that compounds

Engineering time goes back to product instead of ad-hoc reports. Decisions run on current data rather than last month's export. One audited path replaces copy-paste and shared logins, which is a control improvement on today, not a new risk.

Sizing, stated honestly: the two projects most exposed to inbound requests — DATA PLATFORM and AUTOMATION — took 384 issues in the last 90 days (~128 a month), with a further 96 through IT Support. That is the pool this platform draws from, not a count of ad-hoc requests: Jira is organised by product domain, so those totals also include each team's own planned work, and there is no requester-department attribution to separate the two. Adding a label or component for inbound requests would make this measurable within a quarter, and it is the number worth having before anyone asks for a business case.

And the identity and authorization work pays off for every future integration and every audit, whether or not the agent layer above it ever grows. That is the part worth funding even by someone sceptical about AI.

When each half arrives

Being precise about this matters, because the roadmap below is the honest version and the two must agree. The read-side value lands in Phase 1–2. Everything that writes — actions inside systems, durable automations — lands in Phase 3, behind human approval gates. The read side alone removes the dev backlog as the only door into the platform, which is why we do not need to overclaim the rest.

Three gains that are easy to miss — and all measurable

These are second-order effects rather than headline features, which is exactly why they are worth writing down: they accrue whether or not anyone is excited about agents, and unlike the capacity argument above, each one can be measured.

Regulatory change stops being archaeology

Working out what a new KKrL amendment or FFFS requirement actually touches currently means hunting across ~80 repositories and the people who remember. With domain MCPs and the knowledge graph, "what does this rule touch?" becomes a question you ask.

Measure: elapsed time from a regulatory change landing to a complete impact assessment. For a firm that absorbs regulatory change as routine work, this may be the highest-value item in this section.

Audit and subject-access stop being projects

"Who accessed this customer's data in March?" is an investigation today. With the per-call audit log at the gateway it is a query — for FI, for auditors, and for GDPR Article 15 subject access requests.

Measure: hours spent per access-history request, times the number of requests a year. A recurring cost the platform removes as a side effect rather than as a feature.

Incidents resolve faster

Cross-system debugging — logs, service state and data reachable through one governed path — is where these tools are strongest. Shorter incidents also matter directly for our reporting obligations.

Measure: mean time to resolution before and after, on incidents that span more than one system. This is the read-only Phase 1–2 value, available before anything writes.

Two things this proposal deliberately does not claim

SaaS licence savings. It is plausible that some tools exist only because nobody built the internal equivalent, and that a cheap long tail changes that calculation. We have not verified it, so we are not banking it.

Recruiting and retention. Engineers generally prefer not to work in a ticket factory, and "AI-native" reads well externally. Neither is measurable here, so neither belongs in a business case.

The two capabilities we demo

Architecture doesn't sell itself. These two experiences are what make it tangible, and they are fundamental platform capabilities — not side projects. Note the phase label on each.

Capability A · Phase 1

Dashboards from a conversation

Anyone can ask a question of the data they are entitled to see, and get a living dashboard — not a CSV and a promise.

  1. A colleague asks in plain language: "Weekly application volume by channel, and how conversion moved since the June change."
  2. The harness discovers Data Platform MCP tools, reads the schema catalog, and queries — strictly within the user's scopes.
  3. It publishes a dashboard and iterates as the user asks follow-ups. The Data Platform's versioned records make "how did this change over time" a native question.
  4. Every query is logged with who asked, what ran, and what was returned.
Capability B · Phase 3

Automations without a backlog

Anyone can describe a recurring job and get a durable, supervised automation — with the machinery hidden unless they ask.

  1. A colleague describes the job: "Every morning, reconcile yesterday's X against Y and alert the team if they diverge."
  2. The harness authors the script or flow and deploys it through the Windmill MCP, into the workspace that team's work belongs in — the colleague never has to know Windmill exists.
  3. Anything with side effects passes an approval gate before it can run. Windmill provides schedules, retries, and durable execution.
  4. The user follows progress and outcomes in plain language; the harness surfaces failures and fixes on request. Implementation details stay out of the way.

Where the boundary sits

The harness handles interactive orchestration — the long tail of one-off intent that never justified building anything. Windmill handles durable, deterministic execution — scheduled, long-running, or regulated sequences with retries and recovery. The harness writes workflows; it does not become one. This split is what keeps agent behaviour reviewable and business processes reproducible.

06 · Governance by design

The regulated-firm version of this idea

We are a kreditmarknadsbolag under Finansinspektionen's supervision. That is not an obstacle to this proposal — it is the reason the proposal looks the way it does. A governed MCP platform is how AI access becomes more compliant than the status quo of informal copy-paste, not less. The mapping below is drafted for discussion; compliance and legal sign-off is a Phase 0 deliverable, not an afterthought.

FrameworkWhat it demands hereHow the platform answers
AI Policy (ISMS 1.4) Tiered controls for chat, workflow-integrated, and agentic AI use; approval gates; no customer PII / KYC / AML data or secrets into model context. The platform is the controlled Tier 2/3 path. MCPs redact and mask at source so prohibited data classes never reach the harness. Side-effecting tools sit behind explicit approval gates; production-affecting actions follow the policy's approval chain.
GDPR Purpose limitation, data minimisation, accountability, auditability of access to personal data. Minimisation enforced where it can actually be enforced: field-level allowlists and purpose-scoped tools in each MCP. The gateway's audit log records who accessed what, when, and through which tool — per call.
DORA (EU 2022/2554) ICT risk management, logging and traceability, third-party risk register, resilience. Central tracing at the gateway gives one place to monitor and one place to cut access. Harness vendors (Anthropic, OpenAI) enter the ICT third-party register with defined exit paths — MCP being an open standard is itself a resilience measure. Degraded mode: read-only.
EU AI Act (2024/1689) High-risk classification for creditworthiness assessment (Annex III §5) and for employment and worker management (Annex III §4); prohibited practices under Article 5; transparency; human oversight. The platform's scope is internal productivity. Credit decisioning, scoring and customer risk assessment are architecturally excluded, and so are agent-assisted decisions about employees (see boundaries below). Consequential actions always have a human approver in the loop.
Sector rules (LBF, KKrL, PTL, FFFS) Sound governance and internal control; AML data handling; outsourcing considerations for critical functions. KYC/AML data classes are excluded from MCP outputs by design. Whether any part of the platform constitutes outsourcing of a critical function (FFFS 2014:1) is a question we put to compliance early, with the architecture documented and ready for that review.

Hard boundaries — never through this platform

  • No credit decisioning, scoring, or customer risk assessment. Likely high-risk under EU AI Act Annex III §5 — any such use case requires CTO + compliance review as a separate initiative, and is out of scope here.
  • No automated decisions with legal or financial consequence for customers. Humans decide; agents prepare.
  • No agent-assisted decisions about employees. Recruitment, promotion, task allocation and performance monitoring are high-risk under Annex III §4, and inferring emotions in the workplace is prohibited outright under Article 5. If an HR or people-data domain ever gets an MCP, it ships read-only and excludes these uses.
  • No customer PII, credit, KYC, or AML data into harness context. MCPs redact, mask, or aggregate at source — the enforcement point finally exists.
  • No secrets in tool outputs. Credentials and security configurations never transit the platform.
07 · Roadmap

Read first, widen next, act last

Sequencing is the risk control. We earn each phase by exiting the previous one cleanly, and nothing writes until Phase 3. The durations below are rough indications for shape only — real sizing and sequencing belong to the planning step, not to this document.

PhaseFocusWhat shipsExit gate
Phase 0 Foundation Plumbing only: OAuth authorization server with token exchange; the thin gateway; roles v1 (Workspace groups → scopes); the Connect flow in the harness; the MCP starter kit (template, SDK, review checklist); compliance review of the design. You sign in once and the token swap works end to end — and compliance has seen the architecture.
Phase 1 Read-only MVP Two read-only MCPs — the Data Platform and monitoring/logs — and one real question answered end to end through the governed path. Tool evaluation suite in CI. A colleague outside the platform team gets a real answer, through the governed path, without a ticket.
Phase 2 Read-only main domains Read-only MCPs for the top service domains and key SaaS systems; conversational dashboards generally available; the audit log and an audit dashboard for the platform itself. Still no writes. Weekly active users across multiple teams; zero policy incidents; the audit log answering real questions.
Phase 3 Actions are possible The first time anything writes: side-effect classification standard (safe / reversible / irreversible); write tools behind human approval gates; the Windmill MCP — author, deploy, run, track; the first durable automations. The first action executed in production with a complete audit trail and a human approval at every consequential step.

Beyond Phase 3 the direction is that agent accessibility becomes part of definition of done, every owning team carries an MCP on its roadmap, and the informal access paths get decommissioned because the governed one is easier. That is a direction worth stating, not a phase we are committing to today.

08 · Operating model & the ask

Who owns what — and what I'm asking for

Platform group owns

The gateway, the identity integration, the MCP starter kit and standards (naming, scopes, schemas, side-effect classes, eval requirements), the audit pipeline, and developer support for teams building their first MCP.

Domain teams own

Their MCP: tools, schemas, authorization checks, redaction rules, honesty about consistency. It is a product surface with a roadmap line, like an API — because it is one.

Compliance owns

The guardrail review: data classification per MCP, the outsourcing question, the AI Act boundary, and sign-off gates between phases. Involved from Phase 0, not consulted after the fact.

What I'm asking for

Today — in this room

  • Your input on the architecture and the model — on the reading of the problem as much as on the design.
  • Agreement that this is the direction. Domain MCPs owned by owning teams, a shared identity and authorization foundation, and a thin gateway.

Next — we come back with the detail

  • A detailed design, and then a roadmap with phases and owners, sized properly, with the compliance review running alongside so governance and engineering land together. Planning a roadmap before the model is agreed would be wasted work, which is why it comes second.

Then — it becomes how the teams build

  • Each team owns the MCP for the domain it already owns, the same way it already owns that domain's API and its tests.
  • Agent-accessible becomes part of normal delivery rather than a separate initiative that has to be justified and funded again every year.

This last part is the one worth being explicit about, because it is what agreeing to the direction actually commits us to. This is not a project that finishes and gets handed over to someone to operate. It is a change in what "done" means for a team that owns a domain — and that is a bigger ask than the gateway or the token exchange, so it should be decided deliberately rather than discovered later.

To be explicit about what this is not: I am not asking to staff a team, commit a budget, or lock a date today. Today we agree the direction. The detail and the roadmap come next.

How we'll know it's working

Time from question to answered dashboard (target: minutes, not sprint-weeks). Number of automations running through the governed path. Share of estates agent-accessible. Percentage of AI-mediated access flowing through the gateway rather than around it — the metric that tells us the sanctioned path won.

09 · Objections, answered

The five questions this proposal will get

"~80 services — are you asking for 80 MCPs?"

No. MCPs follow domains, not services. Realistically, 8–12 MCPs cover the estate that matters, and the read-only MVP ships two. The starter kit makes each subsequent one cheaper than the last.

"Isn't giving AI access to our systems dangerous?"

The AI already has access — through people, informally, with no audit trail. This proposal replaces invisible access with governed access: identity-bound, scoped, short-lived, logged, and revocable in one place. Read-only first; every write behind a human approval. The dangerous option is the status quo.

"Why a gateway? Teams could connect harnesses to MCPs directly."

Direct connections mean N×M configuration sprawl, no central policy or rate limits, no single audit log, and token handling reinvented per team. The gateway is thin precisely so it can be boring, and boring is what you want at the choke point where DORA asks "who accessed what?"

"Are we locking ourselves into one AI vendor?"

The opposite. MCP is an open standard adopted across the ecosystem — including both harnesses we already use. Everything we build (MCPs, identity, gateway, audit) is harness-agnostic; the harness is the most replaceable component in the diagram.

"Why not just write more Windmill flows?"

Windmill automates what someone already specified, built, and maintains. The unserved demand is the long tail of one-off questions and small automations that never justify that investment. The harness serves the long tail — and when a job deserves durability, it graduates into Windmill. They compose; neither replaces the other.
